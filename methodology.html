01 · Overview

What the index measures.

The Deflation Index tracks technological cost reductions across four fundamental sectors: computing, communications, energy, and transportation. Each sector is measured using cost-per-performance metrics that capture the underlying force of technological progress.

The DI itself is a weighted geometric mean of these four sector indices, showing how technological deflation compounds over time.

DI = Computing0.294 × Communications0.235 × Energy0.294 × Transport0.176

Why a geometric mean? Technological improvements multiply, they don't add. A geometric mean properly captures that compounding relationship.

Each sector represents a different kind of fundamental progress:

  • Computing (29.4%) — the foundation of modern productivity. Cost per billion floating-point operations.
  • Communications (23.5%) — the network connecting everything. Cost per gigabyte transmitted.
  • Energy (29.4%) — the power for all technology. Levelized cost of solar electricity.
  • Transportation (17.6%) — the movement of goods and people. Cost per kilowatt-hour of battery storage.

Current scope (v3.0.3). Four sectors covering roughly 40% of measurable technological impact. Future versions will add healthcare, education, and housing with rebalanced weights. See the expansion roadmap →

03 · Calculation

How the math works.

The process

  1. Track cost-per-performance for each component ($/GFLOPS, $/GB, $/kWh).
  2. Index to a base year (1990 = 100). As costs fall, the index falls proportionally.
  3. Weight components within sectors using geometric means.
  4. Combine sectors into the DI using the weighted geometric mean formula.
DI = Computing0.294 × Communications0.235 × Energy0.294 × Transport0.176

Why a geometric mean? Technological improvements multiply, they don't add. A geometric mean captures that multiplicative relationship cleanly.

Why these weights?

Sector weights (29.4%, 23.5%, 29.4%, 17.6%) reflect three factors:

  • GDP contribution — the sector's share of the US economy, from BEA data.
  • Enabling effect — how much other sectors depend on this technology.
  • Deflationary force — the magnitude of cost reduction observed.

04 · Robustness

Is this finding robust?

To make sure the headline number isn't an artifact of weight choices, we tested multiple methodologies side by side.

Methodology Weights (C / Co / E / T) Cumulative deflation
Multi-Factor (Primary) 29 / 24 / 29 / 18 −96.25%
Equal-Weighted (Baseline) 25 / 25 / 25 / 25 −95.04%

Key finding. Both methodologies show massive technological deflation (95–96% cumulative). The core finding is robust regardless of weighting choices.

06 · Advanced

Sensitivity analysis.

The headline number uses Multi-Factor weighting. We also publish an Equal-Weighted baseline to show what happens when you remove every subjective choice from the weighting decision.

Why provide an alternative? To demonstrate that the core finding holds regardless of weighting assumptions. We provide our Multi-Factor methodology plus an equal-weighted baseline — the simplest possible approach with zero subjective choices.

1. Multi-Factor weighting Primary

Methodology: Balances three factors — GDP contribution, enabling effect (how much other sectors depend on it), and deflationary force (magnitude of cost reductions).

Weights: Computing 29.4% · Communications 23.5% · Energy 29.4% · Transportation 17.6%

Result: 96.25% cumulative deflation  |  2024 DI: 3.74  |  Annual avg: −9.21%

Use case: Recommended default. Most comprehensive approach accounting for both direct and indirect economic impact.

2. Equal-Weighted Baseline

Methodology: Simple equal weighting across all sectors. No assumptions about relative importance — pure mathematical baseline with zero subjective choices.

Weights: Computing 25% · Communications 25% · Energy 25% · Transportation 25%

Result: 95.04% cumulative deflation  |  2024 DI: 4.96  |  Annual avg: −8.45%

Use case: Zero-assumption baseline. If you disagree with the weighting methodology, this shows the finding still holds.

07 · Limitations

Where this is limited.

We're transparent about where the Deflation Index falls short. Better to acknowledge constraints openly than to overstate capabilities.

Current scope

Sectors covered (v3.0.3)

  • Included: Computing, Communications, Energy, Transportation (4 sectors).
  • Not yet included: Healthcare, Education, Housing, Agriculture, Materials, Software-as-Service.

Coverage. The current four sectors represent roughly 40% of measurable technological deflation, not 100%. We prioritize defensible measurement over comprehensive coverage — better to measure four sectors well than twenty sectors poorly.

Expansion timeline. v4.0 (2026–2027) will add healthcare, education, and housing with rebalanced weights across all seven sectors.

Methodological limitations

Weighting system

  • Current weights based on three factors: GDP contribution, enabling effect, and deflationary force.
  • Equal-weighted baseline tested to confirm robustness (see sensitivity analysis above).
  • Results robust: both methodologies show 95–96% cumulative deflation.
  • v4.0 will add rigorous BLS/BEA-derived sensitivity analyses with expanded sector coverage.

Index construction

  • Geometric mean captures compounding effects (appropriate for exponential trends).
  • Alternative methods (arithmetic, harmonic) documented for comparison.
  • Cost-per-performance focus doesn't fully capture adoption friction, network effects, or regulatory barriers.

Conservative assumptions

  • Where uncertainty exists, we choose more conservative estimates.
  • True technological deflation is likely higher than measured.
  • The index understates the gap, not overstates it.

What we don't measure

  • Free software/services. Difficult to price (Gmail, Google Search, Facebook, etc.).
  • Quality-of-life improvements. Convenience, accessibility, user experience beyond raw performance.
  • Network effects. Value that compounds as more users adopt a technology.
  • Adoption curves. Time lag between technology availability and mass adoption.
  • Regulatory / infrastructure friction. EV batteries got cheaper, but charging infrastructure and regulation add costs.
  • Externalities. Environmental costs, congestion, social impacts.

Why these limitations. We prioritize verifiable, reproducible data over comprehensive coverage. Each limitation represents an opportunity for future improvement and is documented for transparency.

Data frequency

Current

  • Annual major updates (new year's data incorporated Q1–Q2 of the following year).
  • Quarterly minor updates (corrections and refinements).
  • 1–3 month lag from source publication.

Planned

  • Monthly M2/CPI tracking (2027).
  • Real-time dashboard (later phase).
  • Automated data pipeline.

Our commitment to transparency

These limitations don't invalidate the core finding. Massive technological deflation occurred (−96.26% cumulative) while consumer prices rose (+155%). This divergence exists regardless of methodological choices.

What changes with better methodology: the precise magnitude of the gap. Not whether it exists.

We welcome critique. Challenge our assumptions, propose alternatives, suggest improvements. The goal is the best measurement possible, not defending any particular method.
