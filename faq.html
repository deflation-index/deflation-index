Part I

Understanding the index

What is the Deflation Index? +

The Deflation Index measures technological deflation across four fundamental sectors: computing, communications, energy, and transportation. It tracks how much cheaper technology makes these essential inputs over time.

The core finding: from 1990 to 2024, technology delivered 96% deflation across these sectors. Meanwhile, consumer prices rose 155%.

The gap between what technology delivered and what consumers experienced is what the project is trying to understand.

Why 1990 as the base year? +

Three reasons.

Data availability. 1990 is the earliest year with reliable, comprehensive data across all four sectors. Computing performance, communications costs, solar efficiency, and early battery technology all have solid measurement foundations by that point.

Technology inflection. The early 1990s marked the beginning of the modern computing era — Windows 3.0, early internet, mobile phones crossing from luxury to consumer. A natural starting point for measuring digital-age deflation.

Enough time to show exponential effects. 35 years is long enough for exponential cost-reduction curves to show up clearly. Shorter periods would be dominated by volatility; longer ones lack reliable data.

Setting 1990 = 100 as the baseline makes percentage changes easy to track over time.

How is this different from CPI? +

The Consumer Price Index measures what consumers pay for a defined basket of goods. The Deflation Index measures what technology more broadly makes possible.

Scope. CPI tracks a basket of consumer goods. The DI focuses specifically on technological inputs (computing, communications, energy, transportation) that underpin the entire economy.

Methodology. CPI uses hedonic adjustments to try to capture quality improvements but often understates technological progress. The DI directly measures cost-per-unit-performance ($/GFLOPS, $/GB, $/kWh), capturing the full force of technological deflation.

Purpose. CPI aims to measure "price stability" for monetary policy. The DI aims to measure technological progress and reveal where productivity gains flow.

CPI shows +155% inflation over 1990–2024. The DI shows −96% deflation. The distance between those numbers is the interesting thing.

How is the Master Deflation Index calculated? +

The DI is a weighted geometric mean of four sector indices.

Sector weights:

  • Computing (29.41%): FLOPS/$, storage $/GB, memory $/GB
  • Communications (23.53%): data $/GB, voice $/minute, network $/Mbps
  • Energy (29.41%): solar $/kWh, batteries $/kWh, LEDs $/lumen
  • Transportation (17.65%): EV batteries $/kWh, efficiency gains

Formula: each year, sector indices are calculated from cost-per-performance metrics, then combined using the weights above. Geometric means are used because technological improvements compound — they multiply, they don't add.

Result: DI fell from 100 (1990) to 3.74 (2024) — 96.26% total deflation.

For full technical details, see the methodology documentation.

Part II

Gaps & findings

What is the gap between DI and M2? +

The gap measures the divergence between two powerful economic forces.

Technology (DI): pushed prices down −9% annually (96% cumulative deflation).

Monetary expansion (M2): pushed prices up +6% annually (550% cumulative growth).

The annual gap: roughly 15 percentage points per year.

This gap represents the systematic divergence between technological productivity gains and monetary expansion. Technology makes things cheaper; money creation makes them more expensive. These forces move in opposite directions, and the distance between them reveals where productivity gains flow in the economy.

The question: if technology delivered 96% cost reductions, and money supply expanded 550%, why did consumer prices only rise 155%? Where did the remaining productivity go?

Why compare technological deflation to M2? +

M2 is one of several economic indicators the project compares to technological deflation. We also compare to CPI, wage growth, and asset prices. Each comparison reveals different insights about how productivity gains flow through the economy.

What each metric tells us:

MetricMeasuresAnnual rate (1990–2024)
Deflation IndexCore technology costs−9.2%
CPIGovernment-defined consumer basket+2.8%
M2Broad money supply+5.7%

Why the M2 comparison matters. It shows the scale of monetary expansion during the same period that technology delivered massive cost reductions. That helps answer: if technology got so much cheaper, why don't living standards reflect that proportionally?

What about capital formation? M2 expansion can and often does flow into productive investment that drives innovation. The project isn't arguing that monetary expansion never funds R&D and infrastructure — it's measuring the net technological deflation that occurred, regardless of funding sources.

The question being explored is distributional: if technology delivered 96% cost reduction, why didn't consumer prices fall proportionally? That's a different question than "how was the innovation funded?"

Key framing. We're measuring where productivity gains went, not making claims about monetary theory causation. The comparisons to CPI, M2, and other indicators help reveal the distribution patterns.

So where does the productivity go? +

This is the central question. The 14.9pp annual DI–M2 gap compounds into massive divergence — technology makes things cheaper, but consumers don't see proportional benefit. Several forces absorb the difference.

1. Corporate profits. Up 300%+ since 1990, heavily concentrated. Productivity gains often boost margins rather than reducing prices.

2. Asset inflation. Stocks +1,500%, housing +400% since 1990. These aren't captured in CPI but represent where much of the monetary expansion flows. Asset holders capture the gains.

3. Technology platforms. Network effects and pricing power allow tech companies to capture value rather than pass savings to consumers, even as costs fall.

4. Complexity costs. Healthcare, education, and housing costs rising faster than wages. Some productivity absorbed by regulatory, compliance, and distribution complexity.

5. Quality improvements. Better goods at the same nominal price (partially captured in hedonic CPI adjustments, but often understated).

6. International arbitrage. Manufacturing moves offshore (capturing labor cost deflation), but profits stay domestic and flow to shareholders.

The pattern. Productivity gains flow upward to capital holders and asset owners rather than downward to consumers through lower prices.

Why only four sectors? What about housing, healthcare, education? +

Short answer: this version focuses on sectors showing clear, measurable technological deflation. Housing, healthcare, and education are planned for future expansion.

The current four (computing, communications, energy, transportation) were chosen because:

  • Exponential cost reduction. All show 20–40% annual deflation rates.
  • Clean measurement. Objective cost-per-performance metrics ($/GFLOPS, $/GB, $/kWh).
  • Fundamental inputs. These technologies underpin the entire economy.
  • Data availability. Reliable 35-year datasets from authoritative sources.

Why not housing, healthcare, education yet? These sectors show inflation (costs rising) despite technological improvements. They're critical to understanding the full picture, but require different analytical frameworks. Methodologies are being developed to incorporate them in v4.0.

Roadmap. v4.0 (2026–2027) will add healthcare, education, and housing with rebalanced weights across all seven sectors.

Part III

Data & methodology

Is the data public and verifiable? +

Yes. Complete transparency is foundational to this project.

What's public:

  • All raw data — available on GitHub in Excel and CSV formats.
  • All formulas — 900+ verified formulas with zero errors, documented in the Excel files.
  • All sources — every data point includes source citations (FRED, IRENA, BloombergNEF, DOE, FCC, AI Impacts, etc.).
  • Complete methodology — 100,000+ words explaining calculations, assumptions, and quality standards.

Data quality:

  • 400+ data points across 35 years (1990–2024).
  • 900+ formulas verified with zero errors.
  • Average source reliability: 92/100 (A−).
  • All data has quality flags (A/B/C/D grades).

Reproducibility. Anyone can download the Excel files, verify every formula, check every source, and reproduce the DI.

How do I verify the calculations myself? +

Download and check anything. The Excel files on GitHub contain every calculation.

  1. Download. Get any Excel file from GitHub.
  2. Inspect formulas. Click any cell to see the formula (no hidden cells).
  3. Check sources. Hover over cells with comments to see source citations.
  4. Color coding. Blue text = raw data from external sources. Black text = formulas and calculations. Yellow highlight = estimates requiring validation.

An automated verification script (recalc.py) confirms zero formula errors across 900+ formulas. Run it yourself to verify.

Do you have a glossary of terms? +

Deflation Index (DI). Composite measure of technological cost reductions across computing, communications, energy, and transportation (1990–2024). Weighted geometric mean of four sector indices. Fell from 100 (1990) to 3.74 (2024), a 96.26% cumulative deflation.

M2 money supply. Broad measure of money supply including cash, checking deposits, savings deposits, and money market funds. Grew 550% from 1990 to 2024.

CPI (Consumer Price Index). Government measure of consumer price inflation. Rose 155% from 1990 to 2024.

The DI–M2 gap. The divergence between technological deflation (−9.21% annually) and monetary expansion (+5.66% annually) — roughly 14.9 percentage points per year.

Cost-per-performance. Metric measuring price per unit of capability (e.g., $/GFLOPS for computing, $/kWh for energy). Captures true technological progress better than nominal prices.

Percentage points (pp). Absolute difference between percentages. 10% minus 5% = 5 percentage points, not 5%.

For comprehensive definitions, see the methodology documentation.

How often is the index updated? +

Current status. The Deflation Index v3.0.3 is complete through 2024 with zero formula errors and comprehensive 1990–2024 coverage.

Update cadence:

  • Annual updates — full recalculation with new year's data (typically released Q1 of the following year).
  • Quarterly reviews — minor corrections and data quality improvements.
  • Major versions — new sectors or methodology changes (v4.0 will add healthcare, education, housing).

Planned for 2026:

  • v3.1 release with a 2025 data extension (M2, CPI, computing, batteries) for sources that have published.
  • Full v4.0 through-2025 index once IRENA's Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2025 and NREL ATB 2025 land (expected mid-2026).
  • Monthly newsletter with methodology notes and sector updates.

Data freshness. Underlying sources like M2 and CPI are published monthly by the Federal Reserve and BLS. Those get incorporated as they become available.

Part IV

Comparisons & usage

How does this compare to other deflation indices? +

While price indices and hedonic adjustments exist, no publicly available index specifically isolates technology-driven cost deflation across multiple sectors and measures it against monetary expansion. The Deflation Index fills that gap. Here's how it compares to related work:

vs. CPI (BLS). CPI measures consumer prices, not technological capability. Hedonic adjustments attempt to capture quality improvements but systematically understate technology's deflationary force.

vs. PCE Price Index (BEA). Similar to CPI but uses different methodology and weights. Still focused on consumer experience, not technological potential.

vs. GDP deflator. Broad measure of price changes across the entire economy. Useful for macroeconomics but doesn't isolate technological deflation.

vs. Nordhaus (2007) computing price index. Groundbreaking work on computing deflation (which the DI builds on), but limited to computing alone. The DI extends across four sectors.

vs. quality-adjusted price indices. Academic work on hedonic pricing is excellent but scattered across sectors and time periods. The DI provides unified, consistent methodology across decades.

What makes the DI different:

  • Multi-sector scope — computing, communications, energy, transportation in one index.
  • Explicit monetary comparison — DI vs. M2 vs. CPI reveals where productivity gains flow.
  • Long time horizon — 35 years (1990–2024) shows exponential effects.
  • Complete transparency — all data, formulas, methodology public on GitHub.
  • Forward-looking — designed to expand (healthcare, education, housing in v4.0).

Engagement

How can I contribute or provide feedback? +

Contributions, suggestions, and constructive criticism are all welcome.

Ways to contribute:

  • Data improvements. Better sources or spotted errors — open an issue on GitHub with details and citations.
  • Methodology feedback. Challenge assumptions, suggest alternative calculations, or propose new sectors. Scholarly debate is the point.
  • Sector expansion. Domain expertise in healthcare, education, or housing cost dynamics is especially needed for v4.0.
  • Code contributions. Improvements to data pipelines, visualization tools, or site infrastructure — via GitHub pull requests.
  • Share the work. Economists, policymakers, journalists, and investors who might find it useful.

What matters: precision, transparency, intellectual honesty, and constructive engagement. If something looks wrong, say so. If you have ideas to improve the work, send them.
