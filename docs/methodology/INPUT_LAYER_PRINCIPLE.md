# The Input-Layer Principle

**Why the Deflation Index measures technology cost reductions, not consumer prices**

---

## The thesis

Every sector of the economy has two layers. There's the **input layer** — the underlying technologies, materials, and processes that go into producing whatever the sector delivers. And there's the **headline layer** — the price a consumer actually pays at the end of the chain. These two layers can move in opposite directions, sometimes for decades at a time.

The Deflation Index measures the input layer.

It does this deliberately, because the input layer is where technology delivers and the headline layer is where institutional and structural forces decide whether the technology's gains reach the people paying for the output. The gap between the two — measurable per sector, comparable across sectors — is the central observation this project exists to make.

## Two ways to measure deflation

Suppose you wanted to ask "did healthcare get cheaper since 1990?"

The headline-layer answer is the one CPI gives. Look at the prices Americans actually paid for medical care across that period — physician visits, hospital stays, prescription drugs, insurance premiums. Aggregate, weight, and report. The answer is roughly: medical care prices rose about 300% between 1990 and 2024, well above general CPI. By that measure, healthcare did not get cheaper. It got dramatically more expensive.

The input-layer answer is different. Look at the technologies that go into delivering medical care: genomic sequencing fell from $100M per genome in 2001 to under $200 today, a four-orders-of-magnitude decline. Medical imaging cost-per-scan dropped substantially as digital sensors replaced film and AI-assisted reading reduced radiologist time. Telemedicine made consultations dramatically cheaper than in-person visits where it could substitute. Electronic records, lab automation, drug discovery compute — many of the inputs that go into producing healthcare got radically cheaper over the same period.

Both answers are correct. They measure different things. CPI is right about what people paid. The input-layer measurement is right about what technology made possible. The interesting question — the one this index exists to answer — is **why the gap is so large**, and what it tells us about how the savings produced by technology end up distributed across an economy.

## Why we chose the input layer

There are three reasons.

**The headline layer is already measured.** CPI is one of the most carefully constructed economic indices in the world, with a century of methodology behind it and the full weight of the Bureau of Labor Statistics. We have nothing to add by reproducing it. What's missing from public discourse is a comparably rigorous measurement of *the other side of the equation* — the input-cost reductions that should, in a frictionless economy, flow through to the headline. The DI fills that hole.

**The input layer is where technology actually shows up.** When people say "technology has made things cheaper," they're not usually wrong. They're describing a real, measurable phenomenon. But the phenomenon lives at the input layer. The cost of compute, of bandwidth, of solar generation, of battery storage, of genetic sequencing, of robotic actuation — these are the things that fell. The headline layer either inherits those gains or absorbs them somewhere along the chain. Measuring at the input layer is what lets you say "technology delivered" with a number rather than a slogan.

**The gap is the most important data point.** Once you have both layers measured, the difference between them is structural information about the economy. A sector where the input layer fell 99% and the headline layer also fell 95% is a sector where technology's gains reached consumers. A sector where the input layer fell 99% and the headline layer rose 200% is a sector where something — regulation, market concentration, supply constraints, credentialing, financialization — captured the savings before they reached the consumer. Both are interesting. The second is more interesting. And the second is invisible without an input-layer measurement to compare against.

## What this means for the four current sectors

The Deflation Index currently measures four input layers:

- **Computing**: cost per unit of computing capability. Operationalized as $/GFLOPS for general-purpose compute; cross-checked against memory and storage cost curves.
- **Communications**: cost per unit of data transmission. Operationalized as $/GB across consumer broadband, cellular, and fixed-line.
- **Energy**: levelized cost of electricity from solar photovoltaic generation, with cross-checks against onshore wind. Solar is the fastest-falling component of the energy input layer.
- **Transportation**: lithium-ion battery pack cost in $/kWh. Batteries are the binding-constraint input cost for electric mobility, and the curve is what determines whether EVs reach price parity with internal-combustion vehicles.

In all four sectors, the input layer and the headline layer are tightly coupled: a laptop's price is largely determined by computing capability, a cell plan's price by bandwidth costs, a solar installation's price by panel and inverter costs, an EV's price by pack costs. So measuring the input layer also approximates the headline. Computing is essentially all input layer; transportation is more input-layer-driven than headline-driven; communications and energy fall in between.

This tight coupling is why these four sectors were chosen first. The methodological gap between input layer and headline layer is small, so the data is high-quality and the interpretation is clean.

## What this means for sectors not yet measured

The next sectors under consideration — healthcare, education, housing, and others — have a much wider gap between input and headline layers. That's not a reason to exclude them. It's the reason they matter most.

When healthcare is added to the DI, it will measure inputs: genomic sequencing cost, AI-assisted radiology per scan, telemedicine cost-per-consultation, drug discovery compute, electronic records overhead. It will not measure insurance premiums, hospital bills, or out-of-pocket expenses. Those are what CPI measures, and the difference between them and the input layer is the entire point.

When education is added, it will measure online-learning cost-per-credit, AI-tutoring cost-per-hour, digital-textbook costs. It will not measure tuition. CPI measures tuition. The gap is what matters.

When housing is added, it will measure modular-construction cost-per-square-foot, building-automation system costs, smart-home component costs, perhaps energy-efficiency retrofit costs. It will not measure home prices or rent. Those are dominated by land values, zoning, and credit conditions — forces a technology index has nothing to say about. CPI measures those. The gap between the input cost of building a house and the price of buying one is the abundance question, applied to housing.

Each of these expansions is gated on data quality, not on conceptual fit. We will add a sector when we can publish a measurement we can defend with the same rigor as the current four. That's the only criterion.

## The boundary: what input-layer measurement excludes

To be honest about scope, here is what input-layer measurement *does not* tell you.

It does not tell you whether a sector's headline price is fair. The fact that the input cost of housing has fallen, if and when we measure it, will not by itself say anything about whether home prices are too high. Headline prices reflect many things besides input costs — supply, demand, regulation, financing — and none of those are captured by the DI.

It does not tell you why the gap exists. The DI says the gap is there and how big it is. The *causes* of the gap — whether it's regulatory, market structural, monetary, demographic, or all of the above — are downstream questions for economists, policy analysts, and journalists. The index supplies the data; it does not supply the diagnosis.

It does not predict that the gap will close. Some gaps may never close, for reasons inherent to the sector (housing, for instance, is bound to land in ways compute is not). The DI's job is to make the gap visible, not to argue that it should disappear.

## Reading the index correctly

A reader looking at the DI should hold three things in mind at once.

The Deflation Index line is a measurement of technology-driven input cost reductions. It is real, careful, and based on Tier-1 sources. It says *this is what technology made possible*.

The CPI line is a measurement of what consumers actually paid. It is also real, also careful, and produced by a different institution with a different mandate. It says *this is what they paid*.

The space between the two lines is the abundance gap. It is not noise, not error, not interpretation. It is the structural difference between what technology enabled and what reached the people paying. Where it is small (computing, communications), technology delivered. Where it is large (housing, healthcare, education in eventual measurement), something captured the savings before they reached the consumer. Where it is large *and* growing, the question of why becomes urgent.

That gap is what the Deflation Index is for. The input-layer principle is the methodological commitment that makes the gap measurable.
