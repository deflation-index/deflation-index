For fifteen years, the lithium-ion battery has been the most important price curve in transportation. It is also the most important price curve almost no one watches.

The number to keep an eye on is **$/kWh** — dollars per kilowatt-hour of pack capacity. Below $100/kWh, the math of building an electric vehicle changes structurally. The pack is the single largest component cost in a BEV, and at $100 the cost of an EV powertrain falls below the cost of the equivalent internal-combustion drivetrain on a sticker-price basis. Not on a total-cost-of-ownership basis, which already favored EVs years ago. On the *list price*. That's the line where median car buyers stop paying a premium for the clean version.

In 2010, BloombergNEF clocked battery packs at **$1,160/kWh**. In 2025, the same survey put them at **$108/kWh**. BEV-specific packs (which trade some thermal margin for cost) hit **$99/kWh** for the first time in late 2025.

That is a tenfold drop in fifteen years.

## How we got here

Three things happened, each compounding the other.

**Chemistry.** The early 2010s were dominated by NCM (nickel-cobalt-manganese) chemistries optimized for energy density at any cost. The 2020s belong to LFP (lithium iron phosphate) — slightly heavier, slightly less energy-dense per kilogram, but radically cheaper to manufacture, longer-lasting, and free of cobalt's ethical and supply-chain problems. By 2024, more than half of EV batteries shipped globally were LFP.

**Scale.** Global battery production capacity grew from roughly 50 GWh per year in 2015 to over 2,000 GWh per year by 2025 — a 40-fold expansion in a decade. Wright's Law (cost falls a predictable percentage with each doubling of cumulative production) has held tighter for batteries than nearly any other industrial good. BNEF estimates a learning rate of about 18% per doubling.

**Manufacturing learning.** Cell-to-pack designs eliminated module-level packaging. Dry-electrode coating processes reduced solvent use and energy consumption in the gigafactory itself. Inverter and BMS (battery management system) costs fell faster than the cells. Every layer of the stack got cheaper.

The result is the curve that turned a $7,500-pack 1991 EV concept into a sub-$30,000 mass-market BEV. Most of that work happened in the last ten years.

## What $108 means in 2025

A 2025 mid-range BEV with a 70 kWh pack carries about **$7,560** in raw battery cost — roughly the price of a decent used car. In 2010, that same pack would have cost **$81,200**. The pack is no longer the project. It's a line item.

The $99/kWh figure for BEV-specific packs is more important than it sounds. Industry has historically averaged across BEV, hybrid, and stationary applications, which inflates the headline number — stationary storage tolerates cheaper, lower-density chemistries. The BEV-specific cut tells you what an actual electric car maker is paying for the pack going into a 2026 model-year vehicle. That number went under $100 in 2025.

For the Deflation Index, the transportation sector — anchored on lithium-ion battery cost — sat at **13.53** at the 2025 early read, vs. its 2010 baseline of 100. An 86% drop in fifteen years. Steeper than communications. Approaching computing.

## What's next: $50/kWh

BloombergNEF's long-run forecast puts battery packs at roughly **$50/kWh by 2030**. That would be another ~50% drop in five years. If it holds, the math gets stranger. At $50/kWh:

- A 70 kWh pack costs **$3,500**. EVs become the structurally cheaper vehicle by a wide margin, before incentives, before fuel savings.
- Stationary storage becomes economic at scale for daily load-shifting, not just frequency response. The duck curve gets eaten by household batteries.
- Long-haul electric trucks hit cost parity with diesel. Air taxis become possible. Marine electrification re-opens.

The 2030 number isn't certain — it depends on continued investment in mining, refining, and gigafactory expansion, all of which face geopolitical and environmental headwinds. But the trajectory is *built in* at this point, in a way that 2010 wasn't. The supply curve is past the inflection.

The Deflation Index transportation sector, on a 1990 = 100 basis, would land somewhere near **6** at $50/kWh. A 94% drop over forty years.

That's the curve. That's why $108 in 2025 matters. And that's why $99 — for the cars actually being built — is the number worth circling on the calendar.
