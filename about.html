About

Why this exists.

Technology got dramatically cheaper over the last three decades. Life didn't. The Deflation Index is an open research project that measures the first half of that sentence rigorously, so we can think more clearly about the second.

01 · The question

What happened to the abundance?

Computing power increased about 100,000x since 1990. Solar electricity fell roughly 90%. Data transmission became nearly free. These aren't opinions — they're measurable cost reductions in dollars per unit of capability: $/GFLOPS, $/kWh, $/GB.

And yet living standards don't feel like they reflect a hundred-thousand-fold improvement in anything. That's the gap this project is interested in.

From 1990 to 2024, technology delivered roughly 96% cumulative deflation across computing, communications, energy, and transportation. Over the same period, consumer prices rose about 155% and the broad money supply expanded 550%. The distance between those three numbers is the story.

This is measurement, not theory. Every number is sourced from a government agency, an international body, or an industry survey. Every formula is in a public Excel workbook. Every assumption is documented so you can disagree with it precisely instead of in general.

02 · How it's measured

Four sectors, tracked for 35 years.

The index tracks cost-per-performance across four fundamental sectors — computing, communications, energy, and transportation — weighted by economic importance and combined using a geometric mean to capture compounding progress. Full methodology is here.

Primary sources: Federal Reserve (M2), Bureau of Labor Statistics (CPI), IRENA (solar), BloombergNEF (batteries), DOE (energy, transportation), FCC (communications), Epoch AI and AI Impacts (computing). Average reliability grade across sources: 92/100.

Conservative by design. Where uncertainty exists, the more conservative number wins. The true technological deflation is almost certainly higher than measured. The index understates the gap, not the other way around.

03 · Principles

Four things this tries to be.

Measurement over theory

Track the data, not a narrative. The index captures systematic patterns across decades, not cherry-picked moments. When the numbers change, the conclusion changes.

Transparency over authority

Every source cited, every formula visible, every assumption documented. Trust should come from verification, not credentials.

Analysis over accusation

Describe mechanisms, not motives. The system can be measured without being moralized. Let the data speak and let readers draw their own conclusions.

Open over closed

Public research, accessible data. Academic use, journalistic citation, policy analysis, critical scrutiny — all encouraged. Knowledge compounds when shared.

04 · Vision

What this could become.

CPI measures consumer inflation. The Deflation Index is an attempt to measure technological deflation with comparable rigor — and the gap between the two is a useful signal for investment, policy, and political economy.

Technology is one of the primary drivers of human prosperity. Understanding where those gains flow — to consumers, to capital, to asset prices, to the accumulated complexity of daily life — matters. The index is never finished, but it's designed to compound: more sectors, better data, stronger methodology, refined by critical feedback.

05 · Who's behind this

A small, open research effort.

The Deflation Index is an independent analytical initiative combining economics, data science, and systems thinking. It's currently a small effort focused on making every number defensible and every source credible.

The project is actively looking for:

  • Domain experts. Healthcare economists, education researchers, housing analysts for the v4.0 sector expansion.
  • Data contributors. Anyone who can help extend coverage or improve a source citation.
  • Research collaborators. Academics working on adjacent questions — combining efforts is better than parallel ones.
  • Critics. If you see methodological flaws or have better data, open an issue or pull request.
06 · Contribute

How to get involved.

Everything is on GitHub. Found an error? Open an issue. Want to fix something yourself? Send a pull request. Have expertise in a new sector or better primary data? Email the project or start a discussion on the repo.

Contributions should include source citations. High-quality, quantifiable, and independently verifiable data is the bar — speculative or promotional content isn't a fit.
